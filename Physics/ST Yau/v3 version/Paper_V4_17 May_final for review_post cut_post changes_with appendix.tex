\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

We present \texttt{SIMON}, a hybrid integrator combining a second-order
leapfrog scheme with a learned scalar pairwise force correction and two
physical constraints: an analytic force direction that preserves Newtonian
radial symmetry, and adaptive sub-stepping that resolves close gravitational
encounters. Through a controlled four-run ablation study---removing each
component in isolation---we show that long-horizon orbital stability over
100 years depends on these structural constraints: adaptive sub-stepping
eliminates the timestep-frontier ejection failure ($105\times$ worst-case
frontier error reduction), while the learned-direction baseline demonstrates,
for the tested initial condition, that replacing analytic direction with a
neural approximation produces unbounded trajectories. The neural network
correction itself provides an $8.5\%$ accuracy improvement without
contributing to stability. Across seven initial conditions spanning
near-circular to tight-binary dynamical regimes, \texttt{SIMON} maintains
bounded dynamics for all stable configurations (mean divergence rate
$\lambda = 0.092 \pm 0.068\ \mathrm{yr}^{-1}$) and correctly identifies
physical instabilities in all ejecting configurations; $96\%$ bounded/ejected
agreement across 500 randomly generated configurations confirms physically
trustworthy characterisation beyond the hand-selected regimes. Energy drift
is attributable to the leapfrog integration scheme rather than the neural
correction; an energy-drift sweep defines the feasible operating timestep
range for a given configuration, and the speed--accuracy frontier then
identifies the optimal operating point within that range. Applied to the
tested configurations, this methodology yields $1.1\times$ to $2.2\times$
speedup over \texttt{ias15}. Validation on the real Sun--Earth--Moon system
using NASA/JPL Horizons initial conditions confirms that bounded dynamics and
the essential role of adaptive sub-stepping extend to real astronomical data.
These results demonstrate that long-horizon stability in neural gravitational
integrators requires two structurally enforced physical constraints; an
integrator built on this principle reliably characterises three-body dynamics
and systematically finds the optimal operating parameters for any given
configuration class.

\vspace{0.5cm}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Three-Body Problem; Neural Networks;
Leapfrog Integration; Adaptive Sub-Stepping; Chaotic Dynamics; Gravitational N-Body Simulation; Lyapunov Divergence

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

The Three-Body Problem---the gravitational dynamics of three mutually
interacting massive bodies---is a classical problem in physics because
its trajectories are generally chaotic and highly sensitive to initial
conditions; long-horizon prediction must therefore be performed
numerically. This remains important in astrophysical applications such
as exoplanet stability, stellar cluster dynamics, and spacecraft
trajectory design \cite{gardner2006james}.

High-accuracy numerical integrators such as \texttt{ias15} provide
reliable reference solutions, but their step-by-step structure makes
long simulations computationally expensive \cite{Rein_2014}. Neural or
hybrid methods offer a possible route to faster simulation
\cite{ulibarrena2024hybrid}, but unconstrained learned force models can
lose physical stability during close encounters, where small position or
force errors are strongly amplified \cite{Dehnen2001}.

This study addresses the following research question:
\begin{quote}
\emph{What structural constraints must a neural force-correction
integrator enforce to achieve long-horizon orbital stability in a
chaotic three-body system, and how can such an integrator be used to
find the maximum achievable speedup over traditional solvers for a given
dynamical configuration?}
\end{quote}

To investigate this question, we developed \texttt{SIMON}, a hybrid
integrator combining a second-order leapfrog scheme with a learned
scalar pairwise force correction, analytically enforced Newtonian force
direction, and adaptive sub-stepping during close encounters.

The key contributions of this work are:
\begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{A two-constraint principle for neural gravitational
    integration:} long-horizon stability requires two structurally
    enforced physical constraints---analytic Newtonian force direction
    and adaptive close-encounter timestep resolution. The neural
    correction contributes accuracy but not stability; neither constraint
    can be replaced by the learned component. This principle applies to
    any neural force-correction integrator in a chaotic gravitational
    system.

    \item \textbf{\texttt{SIMON} as a reliable dynamical characterisation
    framework:} built on this principle, \texttt{SIMON} serves as a
    usable tool for characterising three-body configurations: a
    researcher obtains bounded/ejected classification, divergence rates,
    and trajectory behaviour across a spectrum of dynamical regimes.
    Evaluated across seven distinct regimes and 500 randomly generated
    configurations, \texttt{SIMON} achieves $96\%$ bounded/ejected
    agreement with \texttt{ias15}, confirming that these outputs are
    physically trustworthy across the broader configuration spectrum.

    \item \textbf{A methodology for finding the optimal operating
    parameters:} for any bounded configuration, an energy-drift sweep
    across timestep values identifies the range of $dt$ for which energy
    conservation remains physically valid; within that range, the
    speed--accuracy frontier reveals the trade-off between computational
    speedup and trajectory accuracy relative to \texttt{ias15}, allowing
    the user to select the optimal operating point. Applied to the tested
    configurations, this yields $1.1\times$ to $2.2\times$ speedup over
    \texttt{ias15}.

    \item \textbf{Physical consistency of the neural correction:} energy
    drift across all tested configurations is attributable to the
    leapfrog backbone rather than the neural correction, with
    timestep-dependent scaling confirmed independently of NN invocation.
    This isolates the neural component as an accuracy mechanism and
    confirms that it introduces no additional energy error beyond the
    underlying integration scheme.

\end{enumerate}

The paper is structured as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:lit_review} reviews the physics background of

Section~\ref{sec:comp_modeling} discusses computational modelling,

Section~\ref{sec:methodology} defines the \texttt{SIMON} architecture,

Section~\ref{sec:results} presents experimental results across four

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit_review}

The literature relevant to this project lies at the intersection of celestial mechanics,
numerical integration, and scientific machine learning. Classical mechanics explains why
the unrestricted three-body problem is sensitive to initial conditions and difficult to solve
analytically, while numerical-analysis literature shows that long-horizon behaviour depends
strongly on whether an algorithm preserves geometric structure and handles close encounters
robustly \cite{hairer2006geometric,Rein_2015,Rein_2014,boekholt2015reliability}. More recent
machine-learning work shows that neural networks can act as learned simulators or learned
Hamiltonian surrogates, but also that their success depends on the physical inductive biases
built into the architecture \cite{chen2018neuralode,battaglia2016interaction,greydanus2019hamiltonian,gruver2022deconstructing,horn2025generalized}. This section gives the physics background needed to motivate the computational gap addressed by \texttt{SIMON}.

\subsection{From Two-Body Solvability to Three-Body Non-Integrability}

The Two-Body Problem is analytically tractable because the mutual gravitational attraction
between two isolated point masses can be reduced to an equivalent one-body problem in a
central potential. Newton's law of universal gravitation gives the magnitude of the force as
\begin{equation}
F = G\frac{m_1m_2}{r^2},
\end{equation}
where $G$ is the gravitational constant, $m_1$ and $m_2$ are the two masses, and $r$ is their
separation. This framework explains the classical Keplerian orbits used to model systems
such as planets, moons, and satellites \cite{murray1999solar}.

The unrestricted Three-Body Problem is fundamentally different. Each body is accelerated
by the other two at the same time, so the equations of motion are coupled and nonlinear.
For bodies with positions $\mathbf r_i$ and masses $m_i$, the Newtonian equation of motion
can be written compactly as
\begin{equation}
\ddot{\mathbf r}_i =
G\sum_{j\ne i} m_j
\frac{\mathbf r_j-\mathbf r_i}{|\mathbf r_j-\mathbf r_i|^3},
\qquad i=1,2,3.
\end{equation}
Unlike the two-body case, this system has no general closed-form solution. Its behaviour
depends sensitively on mass ratios, separations, and velocities, making numerical simulation
the standard approach for studying generic three-body dynamics \cite{murray1999solar}.

\subsection{Chaos and Long-Horizon Prediction}
\label{sec:chaos}

A central difficulty of the Three-Body Problem is chaos. Small differences in initial position
or velocity can grow rapidly, causing two initially similar trajectories to diverge over long
times. One common way to describe this behaviour is the Lyapunov exponent $\lambda$, where
an initial separation $\delta(0)$ grows approximately as
\begin{equation}
\delta(t) \approx \delta(0)e^{\lambda t}.
\end{equation}
A positive value of $\lambda$ indicates exponential sensitivity to initial conditions \cite{ott2002chaos}.
This idea traces back to Poincar\'e's analysis of the three-body problem, which showed that
gravitational systems can exhibit intricate, non-repeating trajectories rather than simple
closed-form motion \cite{poincare1890sur}.

For this project, chaos has an important methodological consequence. In a chaotic system,
long-term pointwise trajectory agreement is not always the right standard for judging a
simulator, because even small numerical differences can produce phase drift. Instead, a
useful evaluation must also consider qualitative dynamical behaviour: whether a system
remains bounded, whether ejection occurs, whether energy drift is physically explainable,
and whether divergence grows at a controlled rate. This motivates the use of divergence-rate
proxies and boundedness checks later in this paper.

\subsection{Restricted and Unrestricted Three-Body Problems}

The Restricted Three-Body Problem is a simplified version in which two massive bodies
influence a third body whose mass is small enough to neglect. This formulation led to
important results such as Euler's and Lagrange's special solutions and the five Lagrange
points \cite{bistafa2021euler,lagrange1772essai}. These ideas are important in practical mission
design; for example, the James Webb Space Telescope operates near the Sun--Earth $L_2$
region \cite{gardner2006james}.

However, the present work focuses on the unrestricted problem, where all three bodies exert
mutual gravitational forces. This case is more directly relevant to testing neural-assisted
integrators because the system does not reduce to a fixed background potential and can
include strong pairwise encounters. The unrestricted setting therefore provides a stricter
test of whether a learned force model can remain dynamically usable when all interactions
are active.

The key implication for this project is that unrestricted three-body dynamics combine three
features that make long-horizon simulation difficult: nonlinear mutual forcing, sensitivity
to initial conditions, and close encounters where the Newtonian force changes rapidly with
separation. These features make purely pointwise long-term trajectory matching unreliable
and motivate the computational question addressed next: how can a hybrid neural integrator
reduce computational cost while preserving the physical structure needed for stable qualitative dynamics?

\section{Computational Modelling}
\label{sec:comp_modeling}

Since unrestricted three-body dynamics do not have a general closed-form solution,
their evolution must be computed numerically. A traditional integrator advances the
system step by step, so long simulations require many force evaluations, and close
encounters can amplify small numerical errors into large trajectory differences. High-accuracy
tools such as \texttt{REBOUND} and \texttt{ias15} are therefore essential benchmarks for
this project, because they show what reliable long-horizon gravitational integration
should look like \cite{Rein2012,Rein_2014}. At the same time, numerical-analysis
literature shows that long-time reliability is not determined only by local truncation
error: preserving geometric structure and resolving close interactions are central to
physical reliability \cite{hairer2006geometric,boekholt2015reliability}.

Neural networks offer a possible route to reducing this computational burden, but the
role of the network must be defined carefully. Some learned-surrogate approaches train
a model to map from an initial state directly to a future state, thereby skipping some
or all intermediate integration steps. \texttt{SIMON} does not use this direct
state-to-future-state strategy. It remains a timestep-based hybrid integrator, using
a neural network only as a local scalar force-correction mechanism during close
encounters. The aim is therefore not to replace numerical integration entirely, but to
test whether a learned component can reduce computational cost while preserving the
physical structure needed for stable long-horizon rollouts.

This distinction is important because close encounters are the most fragile part of the
problem. Under Newtonian gravity, the force between two bodies separated by distance
$r$ is proportional to $1/r^2$, so the force magnitude and its gradient change rapidly
when $r$ becomes small. A small learned error in position, force magnitude, or force
direction can then produce a large artificial impulse, causing non-physical energy
change or ejection-like behaviour \cite{Dehnen2001}. The central modelling question is
therefore not simply whether a neural network can approximate a three-body trajectory,
but which parts of the Newtonian force law must remain exact for a learned integrator
to remain dynamically usable.

\subsection{Learned Simulators and Research Gap}

Prior work gives three important lessons for this project. First, classical integrators
provide the reliability standard: \texttt{WHFast} demonstrates the value of fast
symplectic long-term orbit integration, while \texttt{ias15} provides a high-order
adaptive Gau\ss--Radau reference method for gravitational dynamics
\cite{Rein_2015,Rein_2014}. Work on the reliability of $N$-body simulations further
shows that chaotic three-body scattering can require very high numerical precision to
obtain converged individual trajectories \cite{boekholt2015reliability}. This makes
close encounters and long-horizon agreement especially demanding tests for any hybrid
method.

Second, learned simulators show that neural networks can approximate dynamical systems,
but they do not by themselves guarantee physical reliability. Neural ODEs learn a
continuous-time derivative field \cite{chen2018neuralode}, while Interaction Networks
and graph-network simulators learn object interactions and roll out multi-body systems
over time \cite{battaglia2016interaction,sanchez2020learning}. In celestial mechanics,
Breen \emph{et al.} showed that deep networks trained on high-precision three-body
solutions can approximate bounded-time three-body evolution at fixed cost
\cite{breen2020newton}. Physics-informed neural networks take another route by imposing
governing equations through the loss function \cite{cuomo2022scientific}, including
recent applications to the three-body problem \cite{pereira2025advancing}. These works
show the promise of learned dynamics, but they do not isolate which components of the
Newtonian force law should be learned and which should remain analytically enforced.

Third, structure-preserving machine learning shows that adding physical inductive bias
can improve extrapolation. Hamiltonian and Lagrangian Neural Networks encode mechanical
structure through energy-based formulations \cite{greydanus2019hamiltonian,cranmer2020lagrangian},
while symplectic or Hamiltonian-inspired architectures aim to preserve geometric
properties of the underlying dynamics \cite{zhong2020symoden,jin2020sympnets,tong2021symplectic,david2023symplectic,horn2025generalized}. However, later analysis also shows that
not every physics-inspired bias contributes equally; some apparent Hamiltonian-network
advantages may arise from other inductive biases such as second-order structure
\cite{gruver2022deconstructing}. This supports a more targeted question: in a chaotic
gravitational system, which physical constraints are actually necessary for stable
long-horizon rollouts?

This question is especially important because exact pointwise agreement over long
horizons is often unrealistic in chaotic systems. A useful learned model should remain
in the correct qualitative dynamical regime, rather than only minimising short-horizon
rollout error \cite{linot2022stabilized,margazoglou2023stability}. For the three-body
problem, this means preserving bounded motion when the reference solution remains
bounded, identifying ejection when the reference system becomes unstable, and keeping
energy drift physically interpretable.

The gap addressed by \texttt{SIMON} is therefore specific: prior work shows that learned
simulators can be fast and that physical inductive biases can improve generalisation, but
it does not clearly test whether a neural force model should learn the full gravitational
force vector or whether Newtonian radial direction should remain analytic while learning
is restricted to a lower-dimensional scalar correction. Nor does it usually separate the
stabilising effect of close-encounter timestep refinement from the effect of the neural
model itself. \texttt{SIMON} is designed around this gap. It combines a leapfrog backbone
with analytically enforced radial force direction, adaptive sub-stepping during close
encounters, and a neural network limited to a scalar pairwise force correction. Its
novelty is methodological: through controlled ablation, it tests which physical
constraints are necessary for dynamically usable long-horizon three-body rollouts.

\section{SIMON: Architecture and Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Method Overview and Reference Solver}
\label{research_obj}

Building on the research gap identified in Section~\ref{sec:comp_modeling}, this section
defines the hybrid architecture used to test whether a neural force correction can be made
dynamically usable in the unrestricted Three-Body Problem. \texttt{SIMON} is a timestep-based
integrator: it does not predict the final state directly from the initial state. Instead, it
evolves the system step by step using a leapfrog backbone, while using a neural network only
as a local scalar correction to the pairwise force magnitude during close encounters.

The reference solution throughout this paper is generated using \texttt{REBOUND}
\citep{Rein2012}, specifically the \texttt{ias15} integrator \citep{Rein_2014}. \texttt{ias15}
is a high-order adaptive Gau\ss--Radau method designed for gravitational dynamics and is
used here as the benchmark for long-horizon trajectory comparison, boundedness/ejection
classification, and runtime comparison. \texttt{SIMON} is not intended to replace
\texttt{ias15} as a universal reference solver. Its purpose is to test a narrower methodological
question: which physical constraints must be enforced for a neural-assisted integrator to
preserve qualitative long-horizon dynamics?

The method has three core design choices:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a second-order leapfrog update provides the integration backbone;
    \item Newtonian radial force direction is computed analytically at every step;
    \item the neural network learns only a scalar correction to the softened pairwise force
    magnitude during close encounters.
\end{enumerate}
This separation between learned and analytic components allows the ablation study in
Section~5.1 to identify which parts of the method are responsible for stability, accuracy,
and computational performance. A compact schematic of the full \texttt{SIMON} workflow is provided in
\hyperref[app:simon_architecture]{Appendix C}.

\subsection{Scalar Force-Correction Model}

For bodies with positions $\mathbf{x}_i$, velocities $\mathbf{v}_i$, and masses $m_i$, pairwise
interactions are computed using the displacement
\[
\mathbf{r}_{ij}=\mathbf{x}_j-\mathbf{x}_i,\qquad
r_{ij}=\|\mathbf{r}_{ij}\|.
\]
The exact Newtonian pairwise force is
\[
\mathbf{F}_{ij}^{\mathrm{Newt}}
=
G\frac{m_i m_j}{r_{ij}^3}\mathbf{r}_{ij},
\]
where the vector direction is determined entirely by the geometry of the two bodies
\cite{murray1999solar}. This exact radial direction is preserved in \texttt{SIMON}; the neural
network is never allowed to learn or rotate the force direction.

Close encounters are numerically difficult because the Newtonian force magnitude changes
rapidly as $r_{ij}$ becomes small. To obtain a smooth local correction target, \texttt{SIMON}
first computes a softened pairwise force using
\[
\tilde r_{ij}=\sqrt{r_{ij}^2+\epsilon^2},
\qquad
\mathbf{F}_{ij}^{\mathrm{soft}}
=
G\frac{m_i m_j}{\tilde r_{ij}^{3}}\mathbf{r}_{ij}.
\]
The network then predicts a scalar correction factor $c_{ij}$ so that
\[
\mathbf{F}_{ij}^{\mathrm{SIMON}}
=
c_{ij}\mathbf{F}_{ij}^{\mathrm{soft}}.
\]
Equivalently, the ideal correction satisfies
\[
c_{ij}
=
\left(\frac{\tilde r_{ij}}{r_{ij}}\right)^3,
\qquad
\log c_{ij}
=
3\log\left(\frac{\tilde r_{ij}}{r_{ij}}\right).
\]
The model is therefore trained to predict $\log c_{ij}$ from the feature vector
\[
z_{ij}=[\log \tilde r_{ij}, \log m_i, \log m_j].
\]
This keeps the learned task one-dimensional in the output while preserving the exact
Newtonian force direction. The architecture is a small multilayer perceptron,
\[
3 \rightarrow 32 \rightarrow 32 \rightarrow 32 \rightarrow 1,
\]
with SiLU activations. This compact design is sufficient because the learned function is a
smooth scalar correction, not a full vector-valued force law.

\subsection{Hybrid Integration and Adaptive Sub-Stepping}
\label{sec:adaptive}

At each macro timestep, \texttt{SIMON} computes all pairwise forces and updates the state
using a velocity-Verlet/leapfrog step. The force calculation is divided into three regimes:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Far field:} if $r_{ij}>500\epsilon=0.15\,\mathrm{AU}$, the NN is not invoked and
    the pairwise force is computed using the exact Newtonian expression.
    \item \textbf{Close encounter:} if $r_{ij}<0.15\,\mathrm{AU}$, the NN predicts the scalar
    correction $c_{ij}$ applied to the softened force.
    \item \textbf{Safety fallback:} if the encounter is extremely close
    $(\tilde r_{ij}<5\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{AU})$, or if the predicted correction lies outside
    the gate $0.2\leq c_{ij}\leq 5.0$, the NN output is rejected and the exact Newtonian
    force is used instead.
\end{enumerate}

This design follows the principle of hybrid scientific machine learning: learned components
are used only where they are intended to help, while physically unsafe outputs are replaced
by analytic force evaluations \cite{ulibarrena2024hybrid}. In particular, the NN correction
changes only the scalar magnitude of the force; the direction remains the exact radial
Newtonian direction throughout.

To resolve close encounters without globally shrinking the timestep, \texttt{SIMON} uses
adaptive sub-stepping. At each macro step, the minimum pairwise distance $r_{\min}$ is
computed. If $r_{\min}<0.05\,\mathrm{AU}$, the macro step $dt$ is divided into
\[
n_{\mathrm{sub}}
=
\min\left(
16,\,
\max\left(2,\left\lceil \frac{0.05}{r_{\min}}\right\rceil\right)
\right)
\]
sub-steps, each of length $dt/n_{\mathrm{sub}}$. Thus, closer encounters receive finer temporal
resolution, while the maximum of 16 sub-steps limits computational overhead. All positions,
velocities, accelerations, and forces are stored and updated in float64; the neural-network
forward pass uses float32 weights, with the scalar correction converted back to float64 before
being applied.

\subsection{Training Procedure}
\label{sec:training}

Training data is generated from synthetic pairwise configurations sampled across the
separation range relevant to the force correction. The target is the analytic correction
$\log c_{ij}=3\log(\tilde r_{ij}/r_{ij})$, so the network learns how to convert the softened
force magnitude back toward the Newtonian force magnitude while keeping the force direction
analytic. Approximately $60\%$ of samples are drawn from the transition region
$r\in[0.5\epsilon,50\epsilon]$, where softening changes the force appreciably, and $40\%$
from the far field $r\in[50\epsilon,10]$, where the ideal correction is close to one.

The fitted value of $\lambda$ used in this work is a divergence-rate proxy rather than a
formal Lyapunov exponent \citep{sep-chaos}. It reflects both the intrinsic chaotic sensitivity
of the three-body system and the accumulated model/integration mismatch between
\texttt{SIMON} and \texttt{ias15}. A perfect reproduction of the reference trajectory would give
$\lambda=0$ in this comparison, while larger values indicate faster separation from the
reference rollout.

The scope of the method is deliberately limited to unrestricted three-body systems and is
not intended as a universal replacement for \texttt{ias15}. The study pursues three goals:
to establish the structural constraints that any neural gravitational integrator must enforce
for long-horizon stability; to demonstrate \texttt{SIMON} as a reliable characterisation
framework for three-body configurations; and to provide a methodology for finding the
optimal operating parameters for a given configuration class.
The limitations of this scope are discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% =============================================================================

In this section we present the experimental results for \texttt{SIMON} across four
themes. Section~\ref{sec:stability} isolates the individual contribution of each
architectural component through a controlled four-run ablation. Section~\ref{sec:generalisation}
demonstrates \texttt{SIMON} as a dynamical characterisation framework across
seven initial conditions and 500 randomly generated configurations. Section~\ref{sec:physical}
examines physical consistency and reproducibility. Section~\ref{sec:computational_performance}
characterises the speed-accuracy trade-off as a separate computational-performance result.

Throughout this section, $\lambda$ denotes the fitted divergence-rate proxy (slope of
$\log\delta(t)$ in the 10--50\,yr window), and ``bounded'' indicates that all three
bodies remain within 10\,AU of the system barycentre at $T = 100$\,yr. The reference
integrator is \texttt{ias15} \citep{Rein_2014}; all initial conditions use masses
$[1.0,\,0.01,\,0.005]\,M_\odot$ unless otherwise stated. At the operational timestep
$dt = 0.04$\,yr, $\lambda = 0.1655$\,yr$^{-1}$ and final RMS $= 1.54$\,AU for the
default initial condition (IC1).

\subsection{Results I --- Stability Decomposition}
\label{sec:stability}

\subsubsection{The Four-Run Ablation Design}

To isolate the role of each architectural component, we conduct a controlled four-run
ablation in which exactly one component is removed or altered per run while the initial
condition IC1, timestep grid, simulation horizon, and evaluation protocol remain fixed. Runs
A, C, and D use the same trained SIMON weights; Run B uses a separately trained
Vector NN baseline because the architecture itself is changed to learn force direction.
The four runs are summarised in
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

\noindent The logic of the design is as follows. Runs A and B establish stability requirements by
showing that removing a component causes ejection. Run C establishes the NN’s role by
showing that its removal leaves the system stable. Run D is the full system, providing
the accuracy ceiling against which Run C is compared. Because Run B is evaluated on the
default initial condition, its trajectory outcome is interpreted together with the
gate-sensitivity analysis rather than as a standalone statistical generalisation.

\subsubsection{Run A: Adaptive Sub-Stepping Is the Primary Stability Mechanism}
\label{sec:run_a}

Without adaptive sub-stepping, \texttt{SIMON} uses a fixed leapfrog step for every
integration step regardless of body separation. At $dt = 0.01$\,yr, a close encounter
occurs whose duration is shorter than the fixed timestep can resolve. The lightest
body receives an incorrectly large gravitational impulse in a single step and is ejected
to $161.7$\,AU at $T = 100$\,yr. With adaptive sub-stepping active, the same close encounter is resolved by splitting the step
into 2 to 16 sub-steps when $r_{\min} < 0.05$ AU, and ejection is eliminated across all tested
timesteps for IC1.

Figure~\ref{fig:run_a_frontier} shows the complete frontier
sweep across five timestep values: $dt = 0.005$, $0.01$, $0.02$,
$0.04$, and $0.08$\,yr. The sweep is run for both conditions. The
worst-case final RMS across the frontier reduces from $161.7$\,AU
before adaptive sub-stepping ($dt = 0.01$\,yr) to $1.54$\,AU after
adaptive sub-stepping ($dt = 0.04$\,yr), a $105\times$ improvement.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{adaptive_error_vs_dt.png}
    \caption{Final RMS position error vs.\ timestep for \texttt{SIMON} with and without adaptive sub-stepping on IC1.}
    \label{fig:run_a_frontier}
\end{figure}

At the operational timestep $dt = 0.04$\,yr---the main timestep used for the
operational comparison---adaptive sub-stepping costs $+28\%$ per-step overhead
($19.21 \to 24.57\,\mu$s), reducing speedup from $1.53\times$ to $1.12\times$
relative to \texttt{ias15}, while reducing final RMS from $1.691$ to $1.543$\,AU
($8.8\%$ improvement) and the divergence-rate proxy from $0.1712$ to
$0.1655$\,yr$^{-1}$ ($3.3\%$ improvement). Both configurations remain faster
than \texttt{ias15}. The reported NN invocation rate is less than $1.1\%$ of pair evaluations
involving close encounters with $r<0.15$ AU. Adaptive sub-stepping uses the
stricter condition $r_{\min}<0.05$ AU, so it is triggered only on a subset of
those close-encounter cases, leaving the majority of steps at the base leapfrog
cost.

\subsubsection{Run B: Analytic Force Direction as a Structural Physical Constraint}
\label{sec:run_b}

In Run B, the analytic force direction $\mathbf{u}_{ij} = \mathbf{r}_{ij}/\|\mathbf{r}_{ij}\|$ is replaced by a learned direction from a Vector NN. The Vector NN uses the same hidden-layer structure as \texttt{SIMON} but receives strictly more information, including the geometric direction components $[r_x/r, r_y/r, r_z/r]$, and predicts a unit force-direction vector. Post-training verification confirms sub-degree directional accuracy, with maximum directional error approximately $0.04^\circ$ across the tested distance range. For the tested initial condition, however, the learned-direction architecture produces unbounded trajectories, reaching final RMS error $83.0$\,AU at $T = 100$\,yr, while \texttt{SIMON} remains bounded at $1.54$\,AU. In plain terms, this run tests whether gravity's direction should be learned approximately or enforced exactly from geometry.

To test whether this unbounded outcome was caused by a single anomalous direction prediction or by accumulated small errors, the Vector NN direction gate was tightened and the simulation was re-run.

The gate-sensitivity result shows that the mechanism is not a single catastrophic misprediction, but the accumulation of small non-radial impulses over many steps. When the gate is tightened to $\cos(\theta) < 0.9999985$, corresponding to an angular threshold of approximately $0.1^\circ$, the ejection timing and final RMS remain unchanged. This threshold is far tighter than the original $30^\circ$ gate and close to the Vector NN's measured sub-degree directional accuracy. 

Thus, the failure is not an outlier that a gate can catch. Instead, many individually acceptable direction errors accumulate during close encounters. A simple scale estimate illustrates the issue: at $dt = 0.04$\,yr over $100$\,yr, there are approximately $2{,}500$ integration steps, and $0.04^\circ$ per close-encounter step can accumulate to roughly $100^\circ$ of non-radial impulse. Because Newtonian gravity is a central force directed exactly along the line connecting two bodies, any learned perpendicular component has no physical basis and can compound into non-physical energy injection in a chaotic system.

Figures~\ref{fig:run_b_divergence} and~\ref{fig:run_b_trajectory} illustrate the physical consequence of the gate-sensitivity finding. During the bounded phase, \texttt{SIMON} and the Vector NN have nearly identical fitted divergence rates ($0.1655$ vs.\ $0.1656$\,yr$^{-1}$), but the learned-direction model later becomes unbounded for this tested initial condition, reaching final RMS error $83.0$\,AU. In Figure~\ref{fig:run_b_trajectory}, the long red dotted segments are escape paths of the Vector NN trajectory, not stable orbital loops.

This result supports the conclusion that analytic force direction is not merely a
convenience but a structural stability constraint. For the tested initial condition,
the learned-direction baseline becomes unbounded despite sub-degree angular accuracy,
and the gate-sensitivity analysis shows that the outcome is insensitive to the specific
gate threshold chosen. Analytic direction therefore removes, by construction, a
non-physical perpendicular-force error mode that a learned direction model can still
introduce during close encounters.

\subsubsection{Run C: The Neural Network Correction Is an Accuracy Mechanism}
\label{sec:run_c}

In Run C, the NN correction is disabled entirely: the correction factor $c$ is fixed
at $1.0$ for all pair evaluations, reducing the force to pure softened Newtonian
gravity. The analytic force direction and adaptive sub-stepping remain active. The
result is a bounded simulation at $1.67$\,AU---not an ejection.

Figure~\ref{fig:run_c_divergence} shows that Run C (No-NN baseline, red dashed) and
Run D (full \texttt{SIMON}, green solid) are visually indistinguishable throughout
the 100-year horizon. Both achieve $\lambda = 0.1655$\,yr$^{-1}$ to four decimal
places. The NN correction contributes $8.5\%$ improvement in final RMS accuracy
($1.67 \to 1.54$\,AU) at the $0.45\%$ of pair evaluations involving close encounters
below $0.15$\,AU, where it is invoked.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{divergence_no_nn.png}
    \caption{RMS position error vs.\ time for full \texttt{SIMON} and the No-NN baseline. Both runs retain analytic direction and adaptive sub-stepping.}
    \label{fig:run_c_divergence}
\end{figure}

This result clarifies the role of the NN correction. Run A shows that close-encounter
stability is provided by adaptive sub-stepping, since removing that mechanism causes
ejection even when the NN correction remains active. Run C shows that removing the
NN correction while retaining adaptive sub-stepping leaves the system bounded, but
increases final RMS error from $1.54$\,AU to $1.67$\,AU. Therefore, the NN correction
is best interpreted as a targeted accuracy refinement at close-encounter steps, rather
than the primary stability mechanism.

\subsubsection{Synthesis: The Two-Constraint Principle}
\label{sec:synthesis}

The four-run ablation establishes a clear decomposition of \texttt{SIMON}'s performance:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Adaptive sub-stepping} eliminates the leapfrog ejection failure at
$dt=0.01$ yr for IC1 (Run A: $161.7$\,AU without sub-stepping versus
$0.847$\,AU with sub-stepping).
  \item \textbf{Analytic force direction} is a structural physical constraint (Run B:
        for the tested initial condition, replacing analytic direction with a learned
        direction produces unbounded trajectories with final RMS $83.0$\,AU).
  \item \textbf{NN correction} is an accuracy mechanism (Run C: removing it leaves
        stability intact but costs $8.5\%$ accuracy).
  \item \textbf{Full \texttt{SIMON}} (Run D) achieves the best accuracy by combining
        all three components.
\end{itemize}

Figures~\ref{fig:simon_traj} and~\ref{fig:simon_timeseries} show the
XY trajectory overlay and coordinate time series for the full \texttt{SIMON} system
(Run D), confirming bounded dynamics for all three bodies over 100 years.

\end{figure}

The primary error mode is phase drift---a gradual timing mismatch that accumulates

This is consistent with the chaotic sensitivity of the Three-Body Problem

\subsubsection{Physically Unstable Configurations}
\label{sec:unstable_ics}

Three configurations produce ejections (IC2, IC5, IC7). In each case, the reference
integrator \texttt{ias15} independently also ejects a body, confirming these are
physical instabilities rather than \texttt{SIMON} failures:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{IC2 (near-equal mass)}: \texttt{SIMON} ejects to $116.9$\,AU;
        \texttt{ias15} ejects to $58.9$\,AU. The $2\times$ post-ejection discrepancy
        is consistent with chaotic amplification of integrator differences after the
        system becomes dynamically unbound.
  \item \textbf{IC5 (high eccentricity, $v = 2.2$, $r = 0.3$\,AU)}: Both
        integrators eject; \texttt{ias15} ejects Body~2 at $t = 34.8$\,yr.
  \item \textbf{IC7 (high eccentricity redesign, $v = 1.6$, $r = 0.6$\,AU)}:
        Both integrators eject; \texttt{ias15} ejects Body~2 at $t = 88.4$\,yr.
\end{itemize}

\texttt{SIMON} does not suppress these physical instabilities. This is expected and physically consistent: the model agrees with \texttt{ias15} on the occurrence of dynamical instability rather than artificially stabilising configurations that
are physically unbound. The post-ejection distances are not expected to match exactly
because chaotic divergence amplifies integrator differences after unbinding. High-eccentricity
configurations with these mass ratios proved intrinsically unstable in both cases
tested: the eccentric inner body transfers orbital energy to the outer body through
repeated periapsis passages, eventually unbinding it regardless of integrator.

\subsubsection{Population-Scale Generalisation Validation}
\label{sec:population}

To confirm that \texttt{SIMON}'s qualitative generalisation is not an
artefact of the seven hand-selected configurations, we additionally
evaluated both \texttt{SIMON} and \texttt{ias15} on 500 randomly
generated three-body configurations. The population was restricted to
filtered hierarchical systems: masses sampled log-uniformly from the
scalar network's training range, inner periapsis above $0.20$\,AU, and
the outer body constrained to remain non-crossing ($q_{\mathrm{out}}
> 1.5\,r_1$), so that all configurations lie within
\texttt{SIMON}'s validated operating regime.
At $dt = 0.08$\,yr, \texttt{SIMON} and \texttt{ias15} agreed on the
bounded/ejected classification for $96.0\%$ of configurations
($480/500$). \texttt{ias15} classified $112/500$ systems as ejected;
\texttt{SIMON} classified $124/500$ as ejected, with $16$ false
ejections ($3.2\%$) and $4$ false bounded classifications ($0.8\%$).
For the $372$ configurations bounded under both integrators, the mean
divergence rate was $\lambda = 0.023 \pm 0.021\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$,
below the seven-IC mean of $0.092\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$ as expected from
the hierarchical filtering, but within the range observed across all
seven dynamical regimes ($-0.019$ to $0.166\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$).
This confirms that \texttt{SIMON}'s bounded/ejected agreement is not
limited to the hand-selected configurations.

\label{sec:energy}

energy. This test has three purposes: to verify that energy drift is
not caused by the learned correction; to interpret IC4's larger drift
value; and to characterise how energy conservation depends on timestep
and initial condition.

\paragraph{Attribution.}
The energy drift is not caused by the neural correction. IC3 and IC4
both have NN fraction $=0.0000$, so the learned force correction is
never invoked during those rollouts. Their nonzero energy drift must
therefore arise from the fixed-timestep leapfrog backbone rather than
from NN-driven force errors.

\paragraph{IC4 interpretation.}
IC4's higher fractional drift at the operational timestep ($13.77\%$
at $dt=0.04$\,yr) reflects its small binding energy $|E_0|=0.0060$ —
the smallest of the three configurations tested. Because fractional
drift is normalised by $|E_0|$, weakly bound hierarchical
configurations show larger relative energy drift at the same timestep.
This is not NN-driven energy injection: the NN is never invoked for
IC4. The rollout remains fully bounded over $T=100$\,yr, consistent
with the multi-IC result (final RMS $=1.60$\,AU).

series across all seven tested timestep values. At $dt = 0.04$ yr (orange), the drift is oscillatory rather than
monotonically increasing, consistent with bounded finite-timestep energy error from a
leapfrog-type backbone. At $dt = 0.05$ yr and coarser, the time series shows a sharp
energy-baseline elevation near $t \approx 10$ yr: the orbit shifts to a higher-energy
configuration from that point and the integration continues on the perturbed trajectory.
The three values at $dt = 0.05$, $0.06$, and $0.08$ yr — $22.81\%$, $24.11\%$, and
$25.18\%$ — cluster tightly together, suggesting that the dominant error is associated
with the same under-resolved encounter rather than gradual accumulated drift. The threshold for IC4 lies between
$dt=0.04$ and $dt=0.05$\,yr. The choice of $dt=0.04$\,yr as the
operational timestep is therefore a conservative design decision for
this particular hierarchical stress case, not a universal requirement.

\subsubsection{Timestep Sensitivity of Energy Drift}
\label{sec:dt_scaling}

Table~\ref{tab:energy_dt_sweep} shows that energy drift depends on
both timestep and initial condition, and the two interact differently
across the dynamical spectrum.

\paragraph{IC3: smooth scaling.}
IC3 gives the clearest monotonic trend, rising from $0.02\%$ at
$dt=0.01$\,yr to $2.52\%$ at $dt=0.10$\,yr with no exceptions. This
is consistent with second-order leapfrog accumulation: a tight-pair
configuration with frequent but well-resolved encounters produces
energy error that scales predictably with timestep.

\paragraph{IC4: threshold behaviour.}
IC4 is the most timestep-sensitive case. Below the threshold, the drift shows second-order-like timestep dependence:
reducing the timestep from $dt = 0.04$ to $dt = 0.01$ yr lowers the maximum
drift from $13.77\%$ to $1.05\%$, a 13.2× reduction for a 4× finer timestep. Above the
threshold, the pattern changes entirely: the three values at
$dt=0.05$, $0.06$, and $0.08$\,yr differ by less than $3\%$ absolute
despite a $60\%$ increase in timestep, reflecting a single
encounter failure rather than gradual accumulation.

\paragraph{IC1: configuration-dependent behaviour.}
IC1 behaves differently from both IC3 and IC4. Its drift remains
below $2\%$ across all seven tested timesteps and is non-monotonic:
the values do not consistently increase with timestep. This reflects
the fact that IC1's energy error at these small magnitudes is
dominated by chaotic trajectory differences between dt values rather
than by systematic integration error. For this moderate-scattering
configuration, operating at coarser timesteps is a credible design
choice with no meaningful energy penalty.

\paragraph{SIMON as a configurable framework.}
These results show that \texttt{SIMON}'s energy conservation
properties are determined jointly by the timestep and the dynamical
regime of the initial condition. The appropriate operating point is
not a single universal value but a design choice that should be
determined against representative initial conditions for the target
problem. The two-step methodology in Section~\ref{sec:perf} provides
a principled basis for making that selection.

\subsection{Results IV --- Computational Performance}
\label{sec:computational_performance}

\subsubsection{Speed--Accuracy Frontier on IC1}
\label{sec:perf}

The speed--accuracy frontier measures the trade-off between simulation
throughput and trajectory agreement with \texttt{ias15} across five
tested timestep values, using IC1 (the default initial condition) as
the benchmark configuration.

\paragraph{Accuracy metric.}
Previous presentations of this frontier used final RMS position error
at $T=100$\,yr as the accuracy axis. In a chaotic three-body system,
however, any two integrations diverge exponentially past the Lyapunov
time ($\sim$6\,yr for IC1), so a single endpoint snapshot at
$T=100$\,yr is effectively a random sample from the chaotic attractor
rather than a reliable measure of integration quality. A coarser
timestep may appear to give lower final RMS simply because its chaotic
trajectory happened to lie closer to the \texttt{ias15} trajectory at
that specific instant.

To reduce this single-snapshot sensitivity, we replace the endpoint
metric with the \emph{time-averaged RMS deviation} over the full
100-year rollout:
\[
    \overline{\mathrm{RMS}}_{0:T}
    = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{k=1}^{N_t}
      \frac{1}{3}\sum_{i=1}^{3}
      \bigl\|\mathbf{x}_{i,\texttt{SIMON}}(t_k)
            -\mathbf{x}_{i,\texttt{ias15}}(t_k)\bigr\|^2},
\]
where the sum is over all $N_t=5000$ equally spaced sample points
from $t=0$ to $T=100$\,yr. This metric uses the full trajectory,
includes the pre-chaotic window where the comparison is most
physically meaningful, and is harder to distort by a single lucky or
unlucky endpoint.

\paragraph{Speed observations.}
The per-step time ranges from $26.9\,\mu$s at $dt=0.005$\,yr to
$29.5\,\mu$s at $dt=0.08$\,yr: a range of only $2.6\,\mu$s across
a $16\times$ change in timestep. This nearly flat profile indicates
that the cost of an individual \texttt{SIMON} step is approximately
constant and is dominated by force evaluation, pairwise distance
checks, and occasional adaptive sub-stepping overhead. The observed
runtime improvement at larger $dt$ therefore comes primarily from
taking fewer macro steps, not from making individual steps cheaper.
For a fixed $T=100$\,yr horizon, the number of macro steps scales
as $T/dt$, so total runtime scales roughly as $(T/dt)$ times the
near-constant per-step cost.

\paragraph{Accuracy observations.}
Under the time-averaged metric, the frontier reveals a clearer picture
than the endpoint metric alone. The $dt=0.005$ and $dt=0.01$\,yr
settings achieve the lowest time-averaged deviation ($0.79$ and
$0.86$\,AU respectively) but run $3$--$7\times$ slower than
\texttt{ias15}. The $dt=0.02$\,yr setting produces the worst overall
combination: slower than \texttt{ias15} and with the highest
time-averaged deviation ($1.12$\,AU), reflecting earlier trajectory
divergence that accumulates across the full rollout. At $dt=0.04$\,yr,
\texttt{SIMON} achieves $1.12\times$ speedup with a time-averaged
deviation of $1.08$\,AU. At $dt=0.08$\,yr, it achieves $2.18\times$
speedup with a time-averaged deviation of $0.99$\,AU --- lower than
$dt=0.04$\,yr, confirming that this result is not a lucky endpoint
artefact but reflects consistently closer trajectory agreement over
the full rollout.

\paragraph{Two-step parameter optimisation methodology.}
The energy-drift sweep in Section~\ref{sec:dt_scaling} and the
speed--accuracy frontier above together constitute a methodology for
finding the optimal operating timestep for any target three-body
configuration. In the first step, the energy-drift sweep defines the
feasible $dt$ range: values for which energy conservation shows
physically valid, oscillatory shadow-Hamiltonian behaviour for the
target configuration. In the second step, the frontier reveals the
trade-off between computational speedup and time-averaged trajectory
accuracy within that feasible range, allowing the user to select their
optimal operating point. For IC1, the energy sweep confirms
$dt=0.08$\,yr as feasible ($1.86\%$ drift, oscillatory behaviour); the
frontier shows this yields approximately $\mathbf{2.2\times}$ speedup
over \texttt{ias15}. For IC4, the energy sweep identifies
$dt=0.04$\,yr as the threshold for valid energy behaviour; the frontier
places this at approximately $\mathbf{1.1\times}$ speedup.
\texttt{SIMON}'s operational speedup is therefore a
configuration-dependent range rather than a single number,
spanning approximately $\mathbf{1.1\times}$ to $\mathbf{2.2\times}$
across the tested dynamical spectrum.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{The Two-Constraint Principle}

The central finding of this work is that long-horizon orbital stability in a hybrid
neural three-body integrator is not produced by the neural network alone. It requires
two complementary physical constraints: adaptive sub-stepping and analytic force
direction. Adaptive sub-stepping operates at the integration level by resolving close
encounters that a fixed macro-timestep can under-resolve. Analytic force direction
operates at the force-computation level by enforcing the exact Newtonian radial
symmetry at every step.

The ablation study shows that these constraints have distinct roles. Removing adaptive
sub-stepping causes an under-resolved close encounter to produce ejection in the timestep
frontier. Replacing analytic force direction with a learned direction also leads to
unbounded motion for the tested initial condition. By contrast, removing the neural
correction while retaining both physical constraints preserves bounded dynamics, with
only a modest increase in final RMS error. Thus, the neural network correction is best
understood as an accuracy refinement, while adaptive sub-stepping and analytic direction
are the main stability mechanisms.

The broader implication is that neural models for chaotic physical systems should not
freely learn quantities whose symmetries are already known exactly. In \texttt{SIMON},
Newtonian radial direction is enforced analytically, and the network is restricted to a
low-dimensional scalar correction during close encounters. This constitutes a general
design principle for neural gravitational integration: preserve exact physical structure
where available, and use learning only for targeted corrections where analytic or
numerical approximations are most fragile. The principle applies to any neural
force-correction integrator in a chaotic gravitational system, not only to the specific
architecture studied here.

\subsection{Positioning \texttt{SIMON} Relative to \texttt{ias15}}

\texttt{SIMON} should not be interpreted as a replacement for \texttt{ias15}. The reference
integrator remains substantially more accurate in energy conservation because it uses
high-order adaptive Gau\ss--Radau integration. \texttt{SIMON}, by contrast, uses a
second-order leapfrog backbone with targeted sub-stepping, so its energy drift is larger
and follows the expected timestep-dependent behaviour of the underlying integrator.

The relevant comparison is therefore not whether \texttt{SIMON} reproduces every
\texttt{ias15} coordinate exactly over 100 years. In a chaotic three-body system, small
model or numerical differences naturally accumulate as phase drift. The more meaningful
question is whether the hybrid method preserves the qualitative dynamical regime:
bounded configurations should remain bounded, physically unstable configurations should
be identified as unstable, and energy drift should remain physically interpretable.

Under this interpretation, \texttt{SIMON} serves two further roles beyond
proving the two-constraint principle. As a characterisation framework
(Section~\ref{sec:generalisation}), it reliably classifies bounded and ejected
configurations, delivering bounded/ejected status, divergence rates, and trajectory
behaviour across the dynamical spectrum --- confirmed at $96\%$ agreement with
\texttt{ias15} across 500 randomly generated configurations. As an optimisation
tool (Section~\ref{sec:computational_performance}), the two-step parameter
methodology finds the maximum achievable speedup for any given configuration:
the energy-drift sweep identifies the feasible $dt$ range, and the speed--accuracy
frontier reveals the optimal operating point within that range. For IC1 this
yields approximately $2.2\times$ speedup at $dt=0.08$\,yr; for IC4,
$dt=0.04$\,yr is the energy threshold, giving approximately $1.1\times$ speedup.

The same interpretation is supported beyond the synthetic initial conditions used in the main ablations. \hyperref[app:real_system_validation]{Appendix A} tests \texttt{SIMON}
on the real Sun--Earth--Moon system using NASA/JPL Horizons
state vectors, while
\hyperref[app:force_level_nn_validation]{Appendix B} verifies locally
that the learned scalar correction reduces close-range force-
magnitude error in its intended operating regime. These checks do not change the main conclusion; they strengthen its physical interpretation by connecting the controlled ablations to a real astronomical configuration and to the force-level role of the NN.

To answer the research question:
\begin{quote}

\emph{What structural constraints must a neural force-correction integrator enforce
to achieve long-horizon orbital stability in a chaotic three-body system, and how
can such an integrator be used to find the maximum achievable speedup over
traditional solvers for a given dynamical configuration?}

\end{quote}

\noindent In the tested three-body regimes, two structurally enforced physical
constraints are necessary and sufficient for long-horizon stability; the neural
correction contributes accuracy but not stability. Built on this principle,
\texttt{SIMON} reliably characterises three-body configurations across the
dynamical spectrum ($96\%$ agreement across 500 randomly generated
configurations), and the two-step methodology finds $1.1\times$ to $2.2\times$
speedup across the tested configurations. The main conclusion is therefore not
that \texttt{SIMON} replaces \texttt{ias15}, but that a neural force-correction
integrator satisfying the two-constraint principle can reliably characterise
three-body dynamics and systematically find the optimal operating parameters for
any given configuration class.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

This work should be interpreted as a controlled methodological study rather than as a
universal neural replacement for high-order reference integrators. Several limitations
define the scope of the present results and suggest natural directions for future work.

First, the ablation study in Section~\ref{sec:stability} is centred on IC1. Although the
multi-IC evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:generalisation} provides evidence that the full
\texttt{SIMON} configuration generalises across several regimes, the causal decomposition
of adaptive sub-stepping, analytic direction, and neural correction should be repeated over
a broader set of mass ratios and initial geometries.

Second, the fitted divergence-rate $\lambda$ is a proxy rather than a formal Lyapunov
exponent. It compares \texttt{SIMON} against \texttt{ias15}, so it reflects both intrinsic
chaotic divergence and accumulated model mismatch. Future work could compute formal
Lyapunov exponents by evolving nearby initial conditions under the same dynamics and
comparing them with the model-to-reference divergence used here \citep{sep-chaos}.

Third, the operating timestep and close-encounter thresholds are tuned for the tested
three-body systems. Larger systems, different mass ratios, or more extreme eccentricities
may require different thresholds, broader training distributions, or additional safeguards.
The pairwise structure of the correction model should extend naturally to $N>3$ systems,
but this has not yet been demonstrated.

Fourth, the present implementation is not optimised for maximum computational
speed. The two-step methodology identifies an achievable speedup range of
$1.1\times$ to $2.2\times$ across the tested configurations, but this range
is constrained by runtime dominated by force evaluation, gating, and
sub-stepping overhead rather than integration arithmetic. Future implementations
could extend the achievable range through vectorised pair evaluation, batching,
GPU inference, or lower-level compiled kernels.

\texttt{SIMON} demonstrates that learned force correction can be useful in chaotic
gravitational simulation when the physically dangerous parts of the problem---force
direction and close-encounter resolution---are constrained analytically. Built on
this foundation, it further demonstrates that a trustworthy hybrid neural integrator
can reliably characterise three-body configurations across the dynamical spectrum and
systematically find the maximum achievable speedup for any given configuration class.

The result confirms that the NN correction performs the local task for which it was
trained. The two-regime structure in Table~\ref{tab:force_error_regimes} reflects the
design of \textsc{SIMON}'s safety gate: for $r < 5\times10^{-4}$\,AU the integrator
falls back to direct Newtonian force, bypassing the NN. The full close-eligible
range ($r < 0.15$\,AU, Row~1) therefore includes a mixed region where the NN
correction is negligible (large $r$) and where the fallback fires (very small $r$),
yielding a modest mean reduction of $1.78\times$. The operationally relevant
comparison is Row~2: in the correction regime where the NN is both eligible and
active ($5\times10^{-4}\leq r < 0.15$\,AU), the mean relative force error falls from
\(3.92\times10^{-2}\) for the No-NN softened baseline to \(1.31\times10^{-3}\) for
\texttt{SIMON}, a \(29.89\times\) reduction. The same pattern holds across
representative in-training mass pairs, supporting the interpretation in
Section~\ref{sec:run_c} that the NN correction is an accuracy refinement rather than
the primary stability mechanism.
